World

Climate talks stall as COP31 draft leaves out fossil fuels

A proposed final agreement at the U.N. climate summit in Turkey has drawn immediate objections because it does not mention fossil fuels or a shift away from coal, oil and gas.

The draft, circulated early Sunday in Antalya, instead emphasizes raising electricity’s share of final energy use to 35% by 2035 and leaves unresolved how much wealthy countries should provide for adaptation and climate finance. Russia backed leaving out explicit fossil-fuel language, while several vulnerable countries and advocacy groups said the text fell short of prior climate commitments.

The dispute came after the summit had already run past its scheduled close, extending a familiar pattern of late-stage bargaining at recent U.N. climate meetings. Negotiators were still haggling Sunday, leaving uncertain whether countries would accept a thinner consensus document or force another rewrite before the conference adjourns.

The negotiations come as the International Energy Agency says the upheaval in oil flows and prices has accelerated changes in how countries think about energy security and electrification. The agency described the disruption as the greatest energy shock in history, with consequences likely to persist alongside climate pressures.

Update Rights groups press for sanctions after world court’s Rohingya genocide ruling

Advocates are urging military and economic sanctions on Myanmar after the International Court of Justice found the state responsible for genocide against the Rohingya.

The ruling itself was covered earlier in the week; the new development is the push for enforcement and accountability after Friday’s reaction from rights groups and U.N. advocates. That leaves the next steps in the hands of governments and the Security Council, where China and Russia could block stronger action. The ruling still adds legal force to years of documentation about the 2017 campaign that drove hundreds of thousands of Rohingya into Bangladesh.

Inflation data and consumer sentiment loom over a market in correction territory

A shortened holiday week will bring fresh readings on consumer confidence, jobless claims and inflation as Wall Street tries to steady itself after a broad selloff.

The Conference Board’s November consumer-confidence survey is due Tuesday, with weekly unemployment claims shifted to Wednesday ahead of Thanksgiving. The backdrop remains a trade-disrupted and energy-shocked economy in which price pressures have spread broadly, even as overall growth has held up better than many forecasters expected.

The data will help show whether households are absorbing higher costs or pulling back further after recent signs of softer retail spending. Markets have also been digesting a sharp retreat in richly valued AI shares, and the recent selloff has pushed the stock market into correction territory.

Analysts have tied much of the drop to a reckoning over heavy investment in artificial intelligence after an extended run-up in valuations. Historically, corrections that stop short of bear markets have taken about 130 days on average to bottom, according to data cited by AP from CFRA.

Update Council hearings turn tense over foster-care failures and waste contracts

Philadelphia officials faced sharp questioning over the death of a 10-year-old girl in foster care, while lawmakers also moved to strip Reworld from a proposed trash contract package.

At a hearing updated Friday evening, Commissioner Kimberly Ali said a gap in Pennsylvania’s resource-family registry helped allow Carprice Williams to care for Malayshia Allford despite earlier warning signs. Councilmember Cindy Bass rejected the explanation as insufficient, calling the death preventable and a failure on a colossal scale.

Williams has been charged with murder after the girl was found dead in a car in Lindenwold, New Jersey. Council members also pressed the administration on foster-home oversight, caseloads and whether more money should go toward keeping families together before children enter care.

In a separate move, a Council committee refused to advance a new waste-disposal deal with Reworld, removing the company from legislation authorizing three contracts. The vote marked a setback for Mayor Cherelle Parker’s sanitation plan and for Reworld, whose Chester incinerator has long drawn environmental-justice objections.

NOAA says warm stretch continued in October as winter outlook favors El Niño

Federal forecasters say the contiguous United States remained above its 20th-century temperature average for a 21st straight month, and El Niño is likely to persist into winter.

NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information said October 2026 continued a long stretch of warmth and cataloged notable weather and climate events around the country. The report offers more context for a year that has mixed persistent heat with volatile regional storms.

The Climate Prediction Center’s seasonal outlook issued this month says December-January-February temperature and precipitation patterns are being shaped in part by El Niño. Seasonal outlooks do not specify local storm tracks weeks in advance, but they guide expectations for heating demand, snow probabilities and water management.
